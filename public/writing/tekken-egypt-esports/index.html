From El Haras El Gomhory to the Tekken Egyptian League: What Competition Transfers, and What Doesn't

By Zyad Husseini · Data analyst and economist, France & Egypt

I played professional basketball for El Haras El Gomhory for six years. Before that, and alongside it, I spent roughly twelve years competing in FIFA and Call of Duty with Reading Ravens Esports, where top-three finishes were routine.

I now compete in Tekken under the tag ELECTRO_SMG, maining Victor in the Tekken Egyptian League on the Tekken World Tour's Middle East and Africa circuit. My most recent result was a 7th place finish at a Tekken Dojo event, which qualified me for the next tournament in the series.

Having moved between three competitive formats, I've become interested in a narrow question: what actually transfers between them?

What transfers: reading an opponent

This is the big one, and it transfers almost completely.

In basketball you learn to read a defender's hips rather than the ball, because hips commit and the ball lies. In Tekken you learn to read what an opponent does under pressure at low health — whether they mash, whether they go defensive, whether they reach for the same panic move every time.

Both are the same underlying skill: building a live model of a specific human being from a small number of observations, and updating it faster than they update theirs of you. Twelve years of FIFA taught me the same thing in a third dialect.

What transfers: composure in decided moments

Basketball games are decided in possessions. Tekken sets are decided in single frames — a launcher that lands or whiffs, a wake-up option guessed correctly.

The physiological response to a decisive moment is identical, and so is the skill of continuing to think through it. That skill is trainable and it is transferable, and it is the single biggest gap I see between talented players who place and talented players who don't.

What does not transfer: the labbing

Here is where the analogy breaks, and where I underestimated the game.

Basketball rewards general athleticism plus schematic understanding. Tekken rewards something closer to research. Every character has frame data. Punishing correctly is not intuition, it is memorisation — knowing that a specific move is minus fourteen on block and that Victor has a fourteen-frame punish for it.

There is no equivalent in basketball to sitting in practice mode for two hours learning the punishment for one move that one character throws out in one matchup. That work is unglamorous, it is closer to studying than to playing, and you cannot substitute reads for it. I lost a lot of sets before I accepted that.

It's also, I'll admit, the part I recognise from econometrics — grinding through specifications until the thing is actually understood rather than approximately felt.

On the Egyptian scene

The MEA circuit deserves more attention than it gets. The Tekken Egyptian League runs genuinely competitive brackets, the Dojo events feed into the wider Tekken World Tour structure, and the level at the top of the Egyptian scene is high.

What the scene lacks is not talent. It is visibility — footage, coverage, archives, the accumulated public record that lets a player outside the region find out that any of this exists. A bracket that isn't recorded may as well not have happened, as far as the wider circuit is concerned.

That's a fixable problem, and it's fixed by people putting their matches somewhere findable. So I've started publishing mine — a broadcast match with live commentary (ELECTRO_SMG on Victor against T-VIRUS on Dragunov, losers bracket, first to two) alongside handheld footage from the venue.

Small contribution. But the archive only exists if people build it.
